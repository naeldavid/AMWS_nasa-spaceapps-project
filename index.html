      03 WHAT IS THIS DEVICE?

      The Astronaut Mission Wrist System

      AMWS is a concept for a ruggedised wrist-worn computer that would sit at the end of a sensor and data network — not a standalone gadget. It could read biosensors on the body, receive telemetry from the spacesuit's life-support system, and exchange data with the habitat, rover and spacecraft, presenting it all as one prioritised display.

      A

      Biosensors

      Could monitor heart rate, blood-oxygen saturation, skin/core temperature estimates, respiration rate, activity and posture, plus stress and fatigue indicators derived from those signals.

      B

      Environmental sensors

      Potentially reads radiation dose rate, suit pressure, oxygen supply, CO₂ partial pressure, temperature and humidity — mostly from the suit's own sensors rather than inside the watch.

      C

      Mission computer

      Fuses physiological, environmental and mission data. Instead of six separate readouts, the crew member sees one readiness score, one risk level and the single most important action.

      D

      Communication system

      The watch talks locally to the suit radio; the suit relays to the habitat, rover or lander, which in turn use spacecraft or orbiter relays and Earth's deep-space antennas. A wrist device cannot reach Earth on its own.

      Why on the wrist?

      • Visible without turning the helmet — suit helmets restrict head movement and peripheral vision.
      • Spacesuits already carry wrist checklists and mirrors; a wrist display extends a proven location.
      • Always with the crew member: inside the habitat, in the rover and during EVA.
      • Works as a backup display if a helmet HUD or tablet fails.

      Human-factors rules used in this prototype

      • Glove targets: on-screen controls ≥ 56 px, with spacing; physical keys for HOME/BACK/ACK.
      • Redundant coding: every status = colour + icon (✓ ⚠ ✕) + word.
      • Hierarchy: emergency → health → life support → comms → mission → secondary.
      • Error prevention: dangerous actions require a sustained hold; release cancels.
      • Low cognitive load: each alert carries one recommended local action.

      04 WHY IT MATTERS FOR MARS & DEEP SPACE

      The farther from Earth, the more the crew must decide alone.

      01

      Communication delay

      Moon: ~1.3 s one way. Mars: roughly 3 to 22 minutes depending on planetary positions, plus periods with no link at all. Crews can't wait for Earth to answer — the device provides immediate local information and pre-defined emergency guidance.

      02

      Human health

      Long-duration flight brings fluid shifts, cardiovascular deconditioning, sleep disruption and radiation exposure. Continuous monitoring could help identify changes early, before they become operational problems.

      03

      Environmental dangers

      Radiation, reduced pressure, oxygen problems, CO₂ build-up and extreme temperatures can develop quietly. A fast-access status display shortens the time from onset to awareness.

      04

      Reduced cognitive load

      Rather than checking the suit, a tablet, the habitat and a dosimeter separately, the crew member sees one consolidated, prioritised view — with the next action already stated.

      05

      Autonomy

      Deep-space crews need local alerts, decision support, emergency procedures, mission information and health monitoring that work fully offline. Earth becomes an advisor, not a real-time controller.

      DestinationOne-way light timePractical meaning
